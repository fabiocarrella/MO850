Part 1 — Can an LLM be a person?

Park et al. (2023) — Generative Agents

  • 25 LLM agents in a Sims-like sandbox: “Smallville”

  • Each agent has: memory stream (natural language log), reflection layer (periodic summaries), planning module (goal-based scheduling)

  • Agents autonomously plan days, form relationships, spread news, coordinate events

  • Assigning one agent the goal “throw a party” → spontaneous coordination across all 25

  • Key result: believable, internally consistent social behavior emerges from the architecture alone

Park et al. (2024) — 1,000 real people

  • 1,052 participants completed qualitative interviews about their lives (demographics, opinions, experiences)
  • Each interview was used to seed a generative agent
  • Agents were then asked to answer attitude surveys, psychological scales, and classic social science experiments

Result: agents replicated participants’ General Social Survey responses with ~85% accuracy

Part 2 — Can an LLM change a person’s mind?

DebunkBot — Costello, Pennycook & Rand (2024)

  • Participants shared a conspiracy theory they found credible, plus their supporting evidence
  • DebunkBot (GPT-4 Turbo) engaged them in a personalized multi-turn dialogue:
    • Acknowledged their reasoning
    • Provided tailored factual counterarguments
    • Remained persistently polite and non-confrontational

Results:

  • Conspiracy belief reduced by ~20% on average
  • Effects held even for beliefs “deeply entrenched and important to identity”
  • Reductions persisted for at least 2 months without further contact

Why DebunkBot works (and why it matters)

  • Conspiracies are idiosyncratic
  • The bot remained patient and non-adversarial
  • It exploited the same personalization logic as microtargeting (and ERI)
  • From mass persuasion to mass intervention

“You could imagine going to conspiracy forums and inviting people to do their own research by debating the chatbot” — Rand (2024)

Zhong et al. (2025) — Bots in climate denialism communities

  • Two Reddit communities focused on climate change denialism
  • Used a combination of manual and LLM-generated messages as replies to posts
  • Key design: insider language —> messages framed using the community’s own vocabulary and logic
  • Bots were ““transparently”” labeled as AI accounts

Results:

  • Evidence-based messages with neutral tone → positive engagement, open discussion among deniers
  • Climate change supporters responded by adding their own evidence
  • But: adversarial responses, account bans, and community resistance also observed

Part 3 — Can LLMs simulate a community?

OASIS — Yang et al. (2024)

  • Open Agent Social Interaction Simulation
  • A generalizable, scalable social media simulator (up to 1 million LLM agents)
  • Designed around real platform architecture:

Five components:

  • Environment Server: tracks posts, users, social graph
  • RecSys: recommends content (by interest, connections, or hot score just like real platforms)
  • Agent Module: each agent has a character description + action space (post, comment, follow, like)
  • Time Engine: activates agents at realistic intervals
  • Scalable Inferencer: parallelizes LLM calls at scale

OASIS — what it can simulate

  • Information spreading (how a post diffuses through the network)
  • Group polarization (how opinions cluster over time)
  • Herd effects (cascading engagement)
  • Larger agent groups → stronger group dynamics and more diverse opinion space

OASIS is a laboratory for studying social phenomena that would be unethical, expensive, or impossible to run on real users

From simulation to real deployment

Real platform data
       ↓
  Profile agents (text → DDR/CCR/topic models → persona)
       ↓
  Run OASIS simulation: what happens if we inject X?
       ↓
  Observe emergent dynamics (polarization, spread, correction)
       ↓
  Design intervention
       ↓
  Deploy carefully (with ethics board, consent, etc.)
       ↓
  Measure in the wild

The antivax case study

  • “Homogeneous” community of 1500 LLM-based antivax agents

  • 1% provax “bots”:

    • Generic provax bots
    • Targeting (ERI) bots

  • “Control” conditions replicated real-life online dynamics

  • Community-level interventions using LLM agents show positive results:

    • Agents’ comments increase both popularity of posters and virality of posts.
    • Both ERI and generic bots reduce user influence within the network
    • Only ERI bots effectively reduce post virality and counteract the “agent effect.”

  • Reception challenges persist

    • ERI comments are more accepted than generic ones.

  • OASIS-style simulations allowed to test alternative moderating solutions

  • Feasibility is hard, but not impossible

Part 4 — Trade-offs and ethics

Methodological trade-offs

Pros of LLM-based generative agent modelling

  • Scale and speed: simulate effects before real deployment
  • Controllability: vary one factor at a time
  • Access: study populations and platforms that don’t give API access
  • Cost: much cheaper than large-scale human experiments

Cons

  • Validity: do LLM agents actually behave like humans? (Park et al.: ~85%, but on surveys, not behavior)
  • Bias: agents inherit training data biases; may overrepresent some populations
  • Circularity: if we train on human data to simulate humans, are we measuring psychology or reproducing it?
  • Opacity: emergent dynamics in OASIS may not map to real-world dynamics

The 2×2 of deployment intent

Beneficial intent Harmful intent
Individual/dyadic DebunkBot: correct conspiracy via dialogue Salvi et al.: personalized persuasion at 81% higher odds
Population/systemic Vaccine hesitancy policy simulation Coordinated antivax bots; election interference

All four cells use the same underlying infrastructure:

  • Persona construction from text
  • Personalized language generation
