What we have covered so far

Why study text computationally?

  • Language as a vehicle for persuasion, misinformation, nudging

  • Psychological constructs (emotion, attitude, personality) leave traces in text

Text as data

  • Text becomes data when features are extracted and structured

  • Sources: social media, news, speeches, surveys — each with tradeoffs

  • Key advantages: correlational, longitudinal, large scale

  • Key risks: non-representative, noisy, ethically sensitive

Word counts & topic modeling

  • Raw frequency → relative frequency → TF-IDF

  • Stopwords and occurrence thresholds: useful but lossy

  • Keyness: statistically sounder than frequency ratios for group comparisons

  • LDA: documents as mixtures of topics, topics as distributions over words

What we have covered so far

Dictionary-based analysis

  • Dictionaries: word lists associated with a construct

  • Types: crowdsourced, expert-generated, corpus-based, thesaurus-based

  • Tradeoff: intuitive and theory-driven, but rigid and context-blind

From embeddings to construct measurement

  • Words in similar contexts share similar meanings (distributional hypothesis)

  • Embeddings: dense vectors where geometry encodes meaning

  • Non-contextual (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText) vs. contextual (BERT, SBERT)

  • DDR: average dictionary word embeddings → cosine similarity with texts

  • CCR: same pipeline but with questionnaire items + contextual embeddings

  • Anchored vectors: project embeddings onto a pole-to-pole axis → reduces construct correlation

  • CAV: data-driven anchored vectors via PLS, works with continuous outcomes

Limits of DDR/CCR

  • DDR: quality depends on dictionary; implicitly encodes associated constructs

  • CCR: depends on questionnaire availability and items quality

  • Both: struggle when constructs are multi-dimensional or definitionally complex

LLMs as a third approach

LLMs bring a few advantages:

  • No need for a reference dictionary or questionnaire

  • Can handle complex, multi-part definitions

  • Produces output and reasoning, which aids interpretability

  • Cheaper than human ratings

  • Better than human ratings?[1] [2] [3]

Limitations of LLMs

LLMs also bring a few disadvantages:

  • Scalability (costs, resource-intensive vs. smaller models)

  • Output quality

  • Transparency and ethics

  • Reproducibility

  • Prompt variations (wording and structure4)

  • Cross-language performances

  • Implicit biases

Best practices for text annotation with LLMs7

  1. Choose your model carefully

  2. Follow a systematic coding procedure

  3. Develop a prompt codebook

  4. Validate your model

  5. Engineer your prompts

  6. Set and report your parameters

  7. Consider ethical and legal implications

Output validation: are our scores meaningful?

The construct validity problem from psychometrics

Operationalization gap: the distance between a theoretical construct and what a method actually captures

Example: measuring “honesty” in political speeches — what would ground truth even look like?

Validation strategies for LLMs

Focus on accuracy and directness5:

Accuracy:

  1. Agreement with human labels

  2. External validity and prediction of ground truth

  3. Prompt brittleness and noise

Directness:

  1. Contamination risk

  2. Shortcut inference

Further validation strategies

When lacking access to ground-truth:

  1. Face validity with obvious examples

  2. Convergent validity with existing lexicons (if possible)

  3. Discriminant validity

  4. “Known-groups” validation

  5. Multi-method triangulation

  6. LLM-as-annotator reliability
